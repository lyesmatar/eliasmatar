01 / MSc thesis · climate & crop modelling

Climate change impacts on agricultural systems in Lebanon

I assessed how a hotter, drier climate would affect wheat — the country’s main cereal crop, about two-thirds of cultivated cereals — through the rest of the century. I processed downscaled regional climate projections (EURO-CORDEX) for two emission scenarios (RCP 4.5 and 8.5) and two future periods (2046–2065 and 2081–2100) against a 1986–2005 baseline, then used the FAO AquaCrop model to simulate rainfed and irrigated wheat yield and water productivity for every agro-homogeneous zone in Lebanon, and mapped the results.

Projections showed rainfall falling 8–30% and temperatures rising 2–4.7 °C. Yield effects varied sharply by region — gains in Mount Lebanon and the South, losses elsewhere, near crop failure in Baalbek-Hermel. That regional split is the core finding: adaptation has to be planned locally, not nationally.

02 / NYGEP · freshwater ecosystems · field & desk research

The Wild Dives — freshwater ecosystem assessment, Ontario provincial parks

A team project through TRCA’s NYGEP co-op (2025) assessing under-documented freshwater ecosystems across Algonquin Provincial Park, Kawartha Highlands Provincial Park and the Queen Elizabeth II Wildlands.

I coordinated the biomonitoring proposal for the Kawartha Lakes region — selecting the study area and delivering the scope, expected outcomes, targets and budget on a tight deadline — then compiled the Kawartha field report and wrote the Algonquin section of the freshwater literature review, synthesising ecological values, conservation efforts and anthropogenic pressures from published sources. The wider project also produced reusable monitoring protocols and GIS maps of invasive-species cover and priority management areas.

03 / Lebanon Reforestation Initiative · habitat suitability · Python

One map for choosing which native trees to plant where

The Lebanon Reforestation Initiative needed a single national map to guide species choice at planting sites. I integrated two datasets: the Important Plant Areas grid (observed species richness and endemism on a 3 × 3 km grid) and MaxEnt species-distribution models for 27 native tree species — models driven by elevation, moisture, solar radiation, bioclimate, precipitation and distance from the sea.

Working in ArcGIS with an arcpy (Python) script, I thresholded each species’ occurrence probability, converted the rasters to polygons, unioned them onto the grid, and computed the mean occurrence per cell — producing a “species count” layer: how many of the 27 species are likely to thrive in each cell. A correlation check against observed richness (Pearson r = 0.4) confirmed the integrated approach held together.

One of several GIS deliverables from a six-month reforestation internship with LRI (2024–25) — alongside the Al Ula habitat map below and a post-conflict soil-contamination model for South Lebanon.

04 / GIS data optimisation · web publishing

Making heavy GIS layers light enough for the web

Also for the Lebanon Reforestation Initiative, on a habitat-categorisation project for protected areas around Al Ula, Saudi Arabia. The classified habitat layers — red sandstone buttes, lava fields, arid thorn woodlands, wadis and colluvial fans, and more, grouped into three categories — were far too heavy to share online, the largest running to several gigabytes.

I cleaned the geometry in ArcGIS with Dissolve and Aggregate Polygons, merging thousands of tiny fragments and closing spurious gaps, which cut the files from gigabytes to megabytes with no meaningful loss of detail. I exported the result to KML and published it as an interactive map anyone can pan, zoom and click.

  • Dissolve & Aggregate Polygons
  • Cartographic generalisation
  • KML export
  • ArcGIS
  • Web map publishing

05 / Esri “Going Places with Spatial Analysis”

Spatial analysis — suitability, hydrology and pattern detection

Maps and analysis workflows I built during Esri’s spatial-analysis course. The capstone is a cougar-habitat suitability analysis presented as a Story Map: it compares potential habitat under a narrower (state-parks) and a broader (wildlife-agency) set of criteria and highlights where likely habitat overlaps with areas of probable human–wildlife interaction, so managers can prioritise field verification.

Other exercises cover downstream tracing and watershed overlay, and hot-spot / outlier detection built as a repeatable model.

About

How I got here

I hold a Master’s in Biodiversity: Management and Conservation of Natural Resources. My thesis combined climate data, crop modelling and GIS to map how climate change could reshape agriculture at a national scale.

Since moving to Toronto I’ve kept building my GIS skills — a GIS Mapping certificate from the University of Toronto, Esri’s spatial-analysis course, and applied work like the Kawartha project maps. This year, on TRCA’s Ravine Youth Team, I’ve been doing field monitoring across the GTA — streams, terrestrial plots, water quality, tree health — which has grounded me in Ontario’s ecology and in recording fieldwork properly through Field Maps and survey reports.

I’ve always wanted to work in nature. I believe that if we do things sustainably we don’t just survive — we thrive, and that’s the work I want to keep doing.
